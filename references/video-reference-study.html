02 / The reusable motion language

The polish comes from controlling attention

One focal event

Tell the eye where to go

Both films make the active area much larger than a normal full-screen recording. The camera arrives at the prompt, selected text or control before the result matters. The surrounding UI becomes context.

Object continuity

Keep a visual anchor

Slack’s document persists as the surrounding view changes. Notion reuses its assistant window. Familiar shapes and positions help the viewer understand the next shot without reorienting from scratch.

Strong hierarchy

Separate claim and proof

Notion uses a brief, readable headline before a concrete interaction. The headline names the value; the UI supplies evidence. Putting both on screen at full intensity would split attention.

Controlled density

Design what is readable

Slack often replaces peripheral copy with bars while keeping important labels and actions legible. These are designed presentation states. The moving cards and clean reframing strongly suggest layered UI/compositing, though the exact asset pipeline is unknown.

Rhythm and rest

Movement needs a landing

Fast entrances are followed by stable states. Smoothness comes from acceleration, deceleration and timing between objects—not simply a high frame rate. Give the result a reading hold before moving on.

A small vocabulary

Repeat with purpose

Blue, red and yellow distinguish Notion’s demonstrations. Pink, green and purple organise Slack’s spaces. Repeated window treatments, illustration styles and transitions make varied content feel like one film.

What looks like “3D”: Slack’s tilted document has depth and shadows around 00:11–00:15. That appearance can come from layered flat artwork in perspective. The reference alone cannot tell us whether it used a 3D renderer. We can reproduce the useful visual effect with much simpler geometry.

Three techniques, stripped down

Use strong deceleration for arrivals and smoother ease-in/out for camera motion. Reserve bounce for playful objects. Add motion blur selectively to fast travel; persistent blur undermines the crisp interface aesthetic.

Recommended for our build

React + TypeScript + Remotion, with SVG assets

Build editable UI states as components, then define what each layer looks like at each video frame. Remotion gives us a repeatable timeline and render; SVG keeps icons and simple illustrations crisp. Put narration, music and effects on the same timeline. This is a proposed implementation, not the software attributed to the reference films. Remotion fundamentals ↗

Start with 1920 × 1080, 30 fps, 120 seconds. That is 3,600 final frames. Reuse components such as a headline, product window, cursor, camera, media card and end card. Store the copy, UI states and cue times separately so a script revision does not require rebuilding every scene.

Use actual product components or captures where they work. Rebuild shots that need independent layers, large zooms or simplified content. Keep concept-only behaviour clear if the hackathon prototype does not yet implement it.

Where After Effects fits

A designer can create the same class of effects with shape layers, masks, precompositions, the Graph Editor and motion blur. It is a strong option for complex character work and hand-tuned compositing. Figma or Illustrator can supply layered designs; neither is proven as an original source here.

Where Rive fits

Use it if a character needs reusable performances or interactive states in the product. A fixed movie only needs a planned performance; simple eyes, face and hand motion can also be authored in SVG. Remotion’s Rive integration synchronises named linear animations—do not assume arbitrary state-machine interaction will render correctly without a test.

Recommended voice-led version

For an unfamiliar hackathon idea, start with a clear voiceover and short on-screen headlines. Draft roughly 210–240 spoken words, then time a scratch read. Leave deliberate pauses for the important result. The voice explains why it matters; the interface shows what happens.

Build a rough animatic with plain cards, temporary narration and temporary music. Adjust the story there. Record the final narration before locking detailed animation timing.

Music + effects + voice

Keep three separate audio tracks. Pick a restrained instrumental cue with a useful opening, development and ending. Align major reveals with musical phrases when that supports the narration. Lower music under speech, raise it in gaps, and use a small vocabulary of clicks, short movement sounds and result accents.

The effect should confirm a meaningful event. Every visual movement does not need a whoosh. Use original or licensed music for the final film; the reference tracks are not production assets for our demo.

A useful first music search is a clear pulse around 95–115 BPM, but that is a creative starting range, not an assertion about either reference’s tempo. Finish with a deliberate musical ending and a readable end card. Check voice intelligibility on laptop speakers at low volume. Frame-based fades and ducking can be encoded with Remotion’s audio controls.

06 / The template for your idea

One story, told in 120 seconds

Use a single realistic task. Show where it starts, what your idea changes and what the user can do with the result. Three decisive product actions are enough to carry the middle.

Distinctive actionWhat happens that is new or unusually useful?Cursor-led action, focused camera move, a visible state change and a short result hold.

ProofWhy should the judges care?A credible before/after or measured result. If unmeasured, describe the benefit without inventing numbers.
CloseWhat should they remember?One sentence, product name, stable end card and musical resolution.

Production order

  1. Turn the idea into one promise and a concrete user scenario.
  2. Write the narration and on-screen copy together.
  3. Storyboard each claim, action and result.
  4. Time a rough animatic with voice and music.
  5. Polish two or three representative shots to establish the look.
  6. Build the complete motion, then mix sound.
  7. Review the exported film for clarity, timing, legibility and sync.

What I’ll need with your idea

  • Who it helps and the painful situation today.
  • The core idea and its strongest “aha” moment.
  • The one workflow you want to demonstrate.
  • What already works: prototype, screenshots or just the concept.
  • Any company branding or hackathon presentation rules.

No polished brief is needed. Describe it naturally; we can turn that into the story and storyboard.

The template is the storytelling and motion system—not a fixed sequence of decorative transitions.

We can reproduce the crisp interface animation, connected transitions and sound structure with code. Bespoke character performance adds art effort. The next step is to fit the system to your idea, then make a timed first cut.
